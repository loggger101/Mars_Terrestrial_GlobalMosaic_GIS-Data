\title{A Co-registered Visible, Thermal-Infrared and Elevation Stack of Mars at $\pm60^{\circ}$: What It Maps and What It Cannot}

\author{Logan M Edwards}
\affiliation{Florida Institute of Technology, OCN 4704 Remote Sensing, Melbourne, FL 32901, USA}
\email{ledwards2024@my.fit.edu}

\begin{abstract}
Viking MDIM~2.1 visible colour, THEMIS day and night thermal-infrared mosaics and the HRSC/MOLA blended elevation
model were co-registered on one grid spanning \pmsixty\ latitude, 86.6\% of Mars, to map impact craters, fluvial
channels and lava flows. The THEMIS mosaics are 8-bit products stretched region by region: dusty and rocky provinces
differ by 47.67~DN in Viking red but by $-0.19$ and $+0.02$~DN in day and night infrared, and against TES
thermal inertia the THEMIS pair keeps only a weak local signal ($|r|\approx0.1$) while Viking colour tracks inertia
planet-wide ($r=-0.36$). On three calibrated material labels, classes of TES inertia, thermophysical units and
dust cover, the thermal pair adds under two points across held-out blocks, never resolved, and nothing once map
coordinates are given; against 100~m THEMIS inertia the night mosaic ranks the ground within 10~km tiles
($\rho\approx0.5$--0.6) but weakly or not across a region. Fill depth yields 465,354 closed depressions $\ge1$~km; 63.2\% of those $\ge5$~km are
catalogued craters, and most craters missed are degraded or breached. Of 969,822 routed channel segments 89.8\%
lie on slopes under $2^{\circ}$. A support vector machine trained on hand-drawn polygons and scored in whole
$15^{\circ}$ blocks it never saw reaches a balanced accuracy of 66.7\% (73.8\% overall), against 61.3\% for the nearest
training polygon's class; the thermal bands add 1.4 to 2.0 points overall but none by balanced accuracy. Revised to
seven classes on a third, rule-made label set, a U-Net scores 87.6\% on 237 held-out polygons, 86.6\% and 85.4\% on two
draws of other blocks, and 49.6\% on a probability sample. No band separates the geologic map's lava contacts or traced margins at Athabasca Valles much better than chance, so lava flows are taken from that map. Three of four hypotheses are not
supported; the fourth is: image gradients find the rims of 104 of 109 large named craters.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

Athabasca Valles, a young channel system south of Elysium, was read for decades as an outflow channel carved by
water released from the Cerberus Fossae \citep{Burr2002, Plescia2003}. High-resolution imaging later showed the
channel floor draped by a single turbulent flood of lava \citep{Jaeger2007, Jaeger2010}, and the USGS geologic map
places it in the Late Amazonian volcanic unit \lAv\ \citep{Tanaka2014}. The case makes the question of this project
concrete: can a co-registered visible and thermal-infrared mosaic separate lava, water and impact landforms, and how
much of the answer comes from the thermal infrared? %% KB §14.4, §41

Thermal infrared is the natural candidate. Rock and coarse debris hold heat through the night while dust cools
quickly, so the diurnal temperature contrast follows thermal inertia, a property of particle size and induration
\citep{Mellon2000, Fergason2006, Putzig2007}. Visible albedo also separates bright dust from dark basalt
\citep{Ruff2002}, and topography carries the morphology of craters, channels and flow fronts. The global mosaics that
hold each of these quantities have been available for years \citep{Archinal2003, Edwards2011, Fergason2018}, but
they come on three different coordinate frames, and a usable stack of them is not a given.

Automated mapping of Mars has mostly taken one landform at a time. Crater detection has moved from handcrafted
image features to convolutional neural networks \citep{DeLatte2019}, which find craters in lunar elevation models
\citep{Silburt2019} and in Mars data \citep{Lee2019}; valley networks were mapped across the planet by an algorithm
that recognises valleys in topography, its output then edited by eye \citep{Luo2009}. Landform classes have been
mapped without labels, from topographic attributes \citep{Bue2006}, and with a convolutional network trained on
fifteen landforms in Context Camera images \citep{Wilhelm2020}. Here the visible, thermal-infrared and elevation
mosaics are stacked first, and the three families are mapped from the one stack. %% KB §69.40

This paper reports the stack, the landform products made from it and the tests run on them. The goals were (1) one
co-registered stack of Viking visible colour, THEMIS day and night infrared and HRSC/MOLA topography on a single grid;
(2) maps of the three landform families, first in a type area and then across the whole extent; (3) a test of whether
the thermal infrared separates surfaces that visible light cannot; and (4) a score for every product against
something it never saw. Four hypotheses were set when the project was proposed: H1, that day infrared shows
lava-flow margins under dust that Viking misses; H2, that fluvial channels have shallower gradients than volcanic ones
and the two separate on gradient against thermal response; H3, that the multi-band composite gives more stable
unsupervised classes than any single input; and H4, that crater rims found from image gradients recover most of the
141 named craters larger than 100~km. The plan that followed asked six narrower questions, Q1 to Q6, from whether
projecting the elevation model changes slope to whether image gradients reveal unit boundaries beyond any one source;
Section~\ref{sec:hypdisc} answers each. Each test below wrote its prediction and verdict rule into its script before it
ran. %% KB §14.1, §47, §51.1, §69.132

\section{Data} \label{sec:data}

\subsection{The source mosaics}

Four global or near-global products were used (Table~\ref{tab:data}). The THEMIS mosaics \citep{Christensen2004,
Edwards2011} are single-band 8-bit display products: the night mosaic's label gives a base of 0 and a multiplier of 1,
with no scaling to radiance or temperature, and the two mosaics were stretched independently (2nd and 98th percentiles
of 35 and 205~DN by day, 58 and 203~DN by night over Ius Chasma). Calibrated thermal inertia cannot be derived from
them. Viking MDIM~2.1 has every one of its 4.25 billion pixels valid, but it is not calibrated albedo either: its
images were high-pass filtered at about 50~km to remove regional albedo and normalised so that equal slopes show equal
contrast, and the colour product is an older colourised mosaic blended over it \citep{Archinal2003, USGS2009Viking}. The Viking bands therefore carry
topographic shading and local albedo under an uncalibrated colour, called Viking colour here. The HRSC/MOLA blend
\citep{Smith2001, Jaumann2007, Fergason2018} is signed 16-bit metres on a geographic grid. %% KB §3, §24.1, §69.50 (W5)

\subsection{Reference data}

Five independent references score the products \citep{Congalton1991}. The USGS Geologic Map of Mars, SIM~3292
\citep{Tanaka2014}, supplies 1,311 unit polygons and is the lava-flow reference. The Robbins and Hynek database
\citep{Robbins2012}, 2020 release, holds 385,049 craters $\ge1$~km. TES thermal inertia \citep{Christensen2001, Putzig2007}, dayside and
nightside at 20 pixels per degree, is the only calibrated thermal product. TES Lambert albedo
\citep{Christensen2001}, from the USGS Astrogeology mosaic at 8 pixels per degree (7,410~m, values 0.06 to 0.32,
fetched for the material-label tests), is crossed with it to give thermophysical units. The IAU gazetteer \citep{Gazetteer} names
141 craters larger than 100~km. Every IAU crater larger than 100~km matches a Robbins crater with a median centre
offset of 1.0\% of its diameter, and the Athabasca Valles floor falls in unit \lAv\ of the geologic map, as
published. %% KB §41, §6, §69.134

\subsection{The analysis extent and the type areas}

No global THEMIS night mosaic exists: coverage stops at $\pm60^{\circ}$. The analysis stack was therefore bounded at
\pmsixty, which removes a third of the rows of a simple cylindrical grid but only 13.4\% of the surface: 86.6\% of
Mars lies within \pmsixty. Relief inside the band equals global relief (29,754~m, Hellas Planitia to Olympus Mons); the
polar layered deposits and ice caps, none of them a target landform, are lost. Two type areas prove methods before
they run at \pmsixty: Ius Chasma ($271$--$286^{\circ}$E, $6$--$13^{\circ}$S), a canyon with the Louros Valles
tributaries, and Athabasca Valles ($150$--$162^{\circ}$E, $4$--$14^{\circ}$N), the flood lava (Figures~\ref{fig:locator}
and \ref{fig:athabasca}). %% KB §14.3, §15.4, §16, §42.1

\section{Methods} \label{sec:methods}

All processing ran headless through \texttt{arcpy} in ArcGIS Pro~3.7 \citep{ArcGISPro}, with GDAL \citep{GDAL} and
NumPy \citep{Harris2020} for raster arithmetic, on a workstation with an Intel i7-8700K, 64~GB of memory and an NVIDIA
RTX~2080~Ti; figures were drawn with Matplotlib \citep{Hunter2007} or exported from the project's map layouts. Every
product and figure has a script that regenerates it \citep{Edwards2026data}. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} outlines the
chain from the source mosaics to the maps. %% KB §2.1, §2.4, §12.1, §69.38

\subsection{Co-registration on one grid}

The four sources span three frames: THEMIS on a central meridian of $180^{\circ}$ (longitudes $0$--$360^{\circ}$E),
Viking on $0^{\circ}$ ($-180$ to $180^{\circ}$E), and the DEM in geographic degrees. A THEMIS window read at face value
lands half a planet away, and elevation derivatives computed on the degree grid carry an undefined vertical factor. A
global Composite Bands run on the unharmonised sources was cancelled four times by 13 September (and a fifth time on 24
September); projected first and bounded to the type area, the same composite took 8.8~s. %% KB §4, §8, §18

The common grid is read from the night mosaic itself: equidistant cylindrical, central meridian $180^{\circ}$, sphere
radius 3,396,190~m \citep{Archinal2018}, at 100~m (G100, $213{,}388\times71{,}130$, 15.18 billion pixels) and as its
exact $2\times2$ aggregation at 200~m (G200, $106{,}694\times35{,}565$). The day mosaic sits on the night grid at an
offset of exactly $+1$ and $+17{,}783$ pixels, so neither THEMIS mosaic is ever resampled. The DEM cell is
199.99999~m on this sphere, so a nearest-neighbour warp to G200 reproduces 262,144 of 262,144 sampled source pixels
exactly, where a cubic warp alters 36\% of them by up to 28~m; terrain is therefore computed on G200 and imagery on
G100. Slope, aspect and hillshade are computed by Horn's method \citep{Horn1981} in metres on the projected grid, which removes the vertical-factor
warning of the degree-grid products. %% KB §28.1, §28.2, §20

The DEM's last column proved defective (nodata in 24,335 rows and wrong values elsewhere; the file also ends 0.46
pixel short of $+180^{\circ}$), which painted a 2~km stripe of spurious classes along $180^{\circ}$. It was replaced
by the mean of its two neighbours and the affected products were recomputed over that column only. %% KB §54

\subsection{The classification stack}

The seven-band stack for classification is on G200 with 97.33\% of pixels valid in every band: Viking R, G and B
(bilinear from 231.5~m), THEMIS night and day infrared ($2\times2$ averages on their own grid), slope in degrees, and
local relief in a $9\times9$ window (1.8~km). Every band is stretched from its 1st and 99th percentiles over \pmsixty\
to 1--255. Elevation itself is excluded: a raw elevation band beside 8-bit bands dominates any distance-based classifier,
and an earlier map built with one was 62\% reproducible from elevation alone. The Viking bands still carry its
shading (Section~\ref{sec:data}). %% KB §31.2, §30.3, §14.2, §69.50 (W5)

\subsection{The diurnal-contrast index}

Since calibrated inertia is unavailable, a relative index was formed: each THEMIS band is scaled by its own 2nd and
98th percentiles to $[0,1]$ and night is subtracted from day, giving a dimensionless value in $[-1,1]$ that is high for
a large diurnal swing (dust-like) and low for a damped one (rock and coarse debris). It is valid only within the area
over which it was stretched. Over Ius Chasma its mean is $+0.0757$ (s.d.\ 0.279), and it is nearly independent of
topography ($r=+0.108$ with elevation, $-0.248$ with slope). %% KB §24.2, §24.3

\subsection{Object-based classes at the type area} \label{sec:segments}

The first maps of the type area were unsupervised, and classifying pixel by pixel leaves speckle. It was measured over
one window across the wall and plateau of Ius Chasma by two numbers: the share of pixels with a four-neighbour of
another class, and the mean run, the horizontal distance to a change of class. Per pixel, 43.13\% of pixels lie on a
class boundary and the mean run is 5.0 pixels. Grouping pixels into mean-shift segments \citep{Comaniciu2002} before
clustering removes speckle only if the classifier works on the segments rather than on their pixels, and then by an
amount set almost wholly by the detail of the segmentation (Figure~\ref{fig:segsweep}). At the finest setting
(spectral and spatial detail 20 of 20, segments of at least 5 pixels) boundary pixels fall only to 35.22\%, with a mean
run of 6.9 pixels. At 14, 14 and 30 pixels they fall to 12.99\%, with a run of 18.6 pixels, about 1.9~km. At 9, 9
and 80 pixels they fall to 6.36\%, with a run of 37.3 pixels or 3.7~km, and the plateau becomes one unit. The middle
setting was adopted, because its run sits just above the 1~km that ten 100~m pixels resolve; the nine classes it gives
are the object-based classes used below. Coarser segments cost more time, not less, and at this setting the
\pmsixty\ stack, 411 times the type area's pixels, would take days, so object-based classification is a type-area
technique in this study and the \pmsixty\ maps are classified per pixel or by the U-Net. %% KB §23.1, §23.2, §23.5, §23.6

\subsection{Closed depressions and channels}

The \texttt{Fill} operation \citep{Jenson1988} floods every closed depression, which is what a fresh crater is
topographically, so fill depth is a crater detector. A depression is kept if it is deeper than 20~m, at least 1~km
across (about ten 100~m pixels, the resolving rule used throughout), no more than twice as long as wide, and fills at
least 0.55 of its bounding box. The thresholds were tuned on two named craters near Ius Chasma: Perrotin (82.8~km) is
found at 77.0~km ($-7.1\%$) and ranks first of 1,321 candidates; Oudemans (124.2~km) is missed, because it is breached
into the canyon and its lowest rim point lies 33~m below its floor (Figure~\ref{fig:craterlimit}). Across \pmsixty\
the detector runs at two scales: the whole extent at 1.6~km for basins $\ge20$~km, and 8,192-pixel tiles of G200 with a
1,024-pixel (205~km) halo for depressions $\ge1$~km. %% KB §26.2, §26.3, §26.4, §28.6

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/crater_limit.png}
\caption{The closed-depression detector on the two named craters it was tuned on. Grey, the HRSC/MOLA elevation; in

Channel centrelines come from D8 flow routing on the filled surface \citep{OCallaghan1984, Jenson1988} with a 5,000-cell (50~km$^2$)
accumulation threshold, ordered after \citet{Strahler1957}. Because \texttt{Fill} turns closed basins into synthetic
lakes, only stream cells where the fill raised the surface by at most 1~m are kept. %% KB §25.1, §25.2

\subsection{Supervised classification and its score}

Five hundred and twelve polygons were drawn by hand in four landform classes across the planet: 302 Crater, 44 steep or
windy hills, 44 lava tube and 122 Normal Ground. Clipped to \pmsixty, they were split by whole $15^{\circ}\times15^{\circ}$
blocks (14 of 39 blocks held out) into 329 training and 183 held-out polygons (Figure~\ref{fig:split}), each polygon whole and assigned to the
block holding its centre \citep{Roberts2017}; one held-out polygon overlaps a training one by 51.5~km$^2$ across a
block edge. A support vector machine \citep{Cortes1995} was trained on the
training polygons (1,000 samples per class) and applied to all of \pmsixty\ at 200~m in 21 tiles of 16,384 pixels.
%% KB §29.2, §31.3, §54.3, §68.2, §68.5

An audit of the 512 labels then revised the scheme to seven classes: Crater narrowed to impact craters, volcanic
plains drawn as Normal Ground, and three new classes, volcanic edifice, apron and fluvial channel. Proposals for each
label and new polygons for the new classes were built from the geologic map, the Robbins database, the IAU names, the
elevation model and the valley map of \citet{Alemanno2018}. Every relabel, check and edifice outline was reviewed by
eye; the apron and fluvial-strip proposals were screened by a rule written before it ran.
On 80 of those proposals, 30 the rule accepted and 10 it rejected in each class, shown on Context Camera images without the
rule's verdict, an automated reading accepted all 27 rule-accepted fluvial strips it decided and 20 of 26 apron pieces
(77\%, 58--89\%), and accepted 4 of 6 and 6 of 8 of the rule's rejects: the screen erred toward strictness. Five
of the apron rejects it accepted are the flat fan-shaped deposits of Arsia and Pavonis Montes, which a slope test
cannot keep; the third label set admits them by unit, not slope, a net 106 more apron labels. %% KB §69.55, §69.56 (D34)
The result, 971 labels, keeps
the same 14 held-out blocks (734 training and 237 held-out polygons; the training labels now lie in 129 blocks, not 25,
Figure~\ref{fig:split}), and a support vector machine and a U-Net were
retrained on it exactly as before. A rule was written before the run for the seven-class map to replace the
four-class one: on the same v2 held-out polygons, (a) its accuracy and \kap\ on the ground both maps can name (v2
classes 1--4) at least the four-class map's, (b) each new class with a user's accuracy above its share of the
held-out area, and (c) Crater, hills and Normal Ground each losing under 5 points of producer's accuracy.
%% KB §68.1--§68.4, §69.2, §69.8

A third label set tests whether more and smaller training polygons help. Every seven-class polygon over
1,500~km$^2$ or wider than 1\textdegree{} was cut on a 0.5\textdegree{} grid, each piece kept only if it passed a
written screen for its class, and polygons were added from the crater catalogue, the geologic map and the elevation
model, by rule and not by eye, giving 10,269 training and 1,652 held-out polygons in the same 14 blocks. The models
were retrained on them; the U-Net's validation loss was unsteady, and the epoch with the lowest, the 19th of 30, was kept.
The loss rose far late in most of the later retrainings that kept the added polygons, but not in all: on the added
polygons alone it peaked at 3.51, and in a second training on the same labels at 2.09 where the first reached 23.25,
so the rise belongs to a training and not to the labels. Each kept its epoch of lowest loss; trained on the cut pieces
alone, like every earlier U-Net, the loss did not rise. %% KB §69.84, §69.86, §69.88, §69.90, §69.93, §69.96, §69.106
Each new map is scored on the 237 held-out polygons, the like-for-like comparison, and on the 1,652. A rule written
before the runs recommends a new map over its predecessor only if on both sets its balanced accuracy and \kap\ are at
least as high, no class loses 5 points or more of producer's accuracy on the 237 polygons and, for the U-Net, volcanic
edifice covers no more of \pmsixty. %% KB §69.49, §69.76

The lead U-Net maps volcanic edifice on 18.0\% of \pmsixty, where the geologic map's edifice units cover 1.1\%. Measured
before anything was changed, the extra edifice is flat, uncratered plains: off the edifice, apron and landslide units,
96.0\% of the 3.2~km cells the map calls edifice have relief of 150~m or less and 63.6\% pass the third label set's own
test for Normal Ground. One retraining was set against that fault. New Normal Ground squares were placed by the third
label set's rule for such a square, unchanged, with one condition added: every cell at least half volcanic edifice on
the published map. Up to 8 went into a block, none into the 14 held-out blocks or into the 14 blocks of the first
other draw, which serve here as validation blocks; 420 squares in 89 blocks were kept, placed by rule and looked at by
no one. The model was trained on that draw's 9,366 training polygons and the 420 squares, with every setting of the
script unchanged, so the draw's own model is its control on the validation blocks. One setting is not the script's:
the training tool finds a learning rate for itself at the start of each training. Five predictions and the rule for
reading them were written before the squares were drawn (Appendix~\ref{app:predictions}). %% KB §69.108, §69.125

Accuracy is the area-weighted (by $\cos$ latitude) share of held-out polygon area given its own class, with Cohen's
\kap\ \citep{Cohen1960} and per-class producer's and user's accuracy \citep{Congalton1991}. Because one class dominates
the held-out area, each map is also given its balanced accuracy (the mean producer's accuracy; \citealt{Brodersen2010}), the mean of the
per-class F1 scores, and its disagreement split into quantity (the wrong amount of a class) and allocation (the right
amount in the wrong place) after \citet{Pontius2011}. Five baselines accompany
the scores: one class everywhere (58.6\% on the held-out set); the class of the nearest training polygon, geography
alone; a Gaussian maximum-likelihood classifier on elevation and latitude alone, trained on the same polygons; the
share of the map that elevation alone reproduces (each 500~m bin given its majority class); and the share of named
craters $\ge5$~km labelled Crater inside $0.7R$ against a ring at $1.5$--$2.5R$. A majority filter removes speckle; its window was set from the resolving rule (about
2~km) rather than from the score, because every score rises with every window when the labelled polygons are large.
%% KB §31.3, §30.1, §32.2, §57, §69.35, §69.50 (A3 (b))

\subsection{Deep learning}

Two models were trained on the training blocks only and scored on the same held-out polygons. A RetinaNet detector
\citep{Lin2017} with a ResNet-50 backbone \citep{He2016} was trained on 3,622 chips of $256\times256\times7$ pixels
(stride 128) holding 5,293 boxes; its score is per polygon, recall being the share of held-out polygons at least half
covered by boxes of their own class. A U-Net \citep{Ronneberger2015} with a ResNet-34 backbone, class balancing and
unlabelled ground ignored in the loss, was trained for 30 epochs on the same chips as pixel labels, in batches of 8
with 10\% of the chips held back for validation and the backbone unfrozen, applied to all of
\pmsixty\ in 243 tiles of 4,096 pixels with a 256-pixel margin, and scored with the same code as the support vector
machine. Because the classification tool works internally in 256-pixel tiles, each predicted with 64 pixels of
padding, the U-Net was run a second time on a grid
offset by 128 pixels, and each pixel was taken from the pass that places it farther from a tile edge. Both models used
the Deep Learning Libraries of ArcGIS Pro, built on PyTorch \citep{Paszke2019}. %% KB §50.2, §51.3, §66.1, §66.6, §66.7, §66.8

\subsection{Uncertainty}

Intervals are 95\% paired bootstrap intervals \citep{Efron1993} over whole held-out polygons (1,000 resamples), over
contacts for the flow-margin test and over 50~km cells for the channel test, so that spatially correlated pixels are
never treated as independent. Polygons in one held-out block share its geology and its errors, so the map scores are
also given with block intervals \citep{Kunsch1989}, which resample the 14 held-out blocks (10,000 resamples, every map on the same
draw). %% KB §69.44

\subsection{Predictions, checks and what repeats} \label{sec:honest}

Three habits kept the scores from being read to fit. First, a run whose result could be read more than one way had
its predictions, and any rule for choosing between maps, written into its script before it ran; each is reported held
or not held, and Appendix~\ref{app:predictions} lists those behind the lead maps. Second, a scoring script writes
nothing unless it first reproduces what is already known: every map it reads must give the accuracy and \kap\ of its
own earlier log within 0.001. Each such check was itself shown able to fail. Before the third label set's maps
existed, the script that judges them was run end to end on earlier maps standing in for them, and then again with a
stand-in rolled half a planet in longitude; the rolled map scored 0.1066 and the script refused it. That rehearsal
also found a fault that would have lost the real result, a crash while the finished numbers were being written.
%% KB §69.76

Third, what a rerun would give is known for each kind of number. Every random draw the scripts make is seeded, so
the splits, the label sets, every score of a map on disk and every bootstrap interval repeat exactly. The training
tools take no seed. The support vector machine repeats all the same: two trainings on one block gave identical
classifier files and maps with none of 18,160,923 pixels different. Random Trees, used only in a superseded
type-area map, does not: two trainings differ on 5.0\% of the pixels. The U-Net does not repeat either, and how far
it moves was measured by training the lead model a second time (Section~\ref{sec:v3}): the two trainings score within
0.74 and 0.87 points of each other and differ on 26.8\% of the ground, so a U-Net's score is firmer than its map. The
trained models are kept, and a map is re-made from its saved model, not by retraining. %% KB §2.6, §69.50, §69.106

The retrainings that test one change at a time (other held-out blocks, no thermal bands, the two halves of the
third label set, a second training) do not classify all of \pmsixty. Each classifies windows only: the extent of a
held-out block's polygons with a 25~km margin, in one pass, where the lead map is the two-pass mosaic. So that the
comparison is like for like, the lead model was run over the same windows the same way. That window map matches the
lead map on a share of 0.8946 of the pixels and gives its scores (87.54\% against 87.62\% on the 237 polygons), a
chip grid at another phase changing single pixels and not the score; every retraining is compared with it and not
with the mosaic. %% KB §69.82, §69.83

\subsection{Who judged what} \label{sec:who}

Three kinds of judgement enter the results, and they are kept apart. The author drew the 512 four-class polygons,
reviewed the proposals of the seven-class scheme, and reviewed 3,791 of the 9,287 type-area candidates as accept,
reject or unsure. Written rules, fixed before they ran, screened the apron and fluvial proposals of the second label
set and placed every polygon of the third (Appendix~\ref{app:rules}); no one looked at those rows to accept them.
The rest was read by an automated process: the remaining candidate channels at Athabasca Valles and the origin of each accepted channel, the lava
margins traced on the Context Camera mosaic, the probability sample of Section~\ref{sec:sample}, and six edifice
polygons outside Tharsis. %% KB §69.33, §69.55, §69.105

Each of the automated readings was given a check, registered before it began, and the checks are reported whether
or not they passed. The process read candidates from contact sheets that show each one in four panels at 100~m (local relief,
a hillshade, Viking colour and day infrared) with no review field, index or slope shown. Blind on 48 candidates the
author had already judged, it agreed with the author at chance (46.5\%, \kap\,0.08), so the candidate review stayed
the author's. On the hidden calibration points of the probability sample, 35 points inside the author's own
polygons, it scored 66.7\%, \kap\,0.60, under the gate set for it. Its channel review and its channel origins failed
their repeat checks as well. At the author's decision those readings are in the data all the same, each row marked
as automated, and no decision of the author's is overwritten by one; the paper gives each with its measured
agreement and says so wherever a number rests on them. The traced margins were read a second time by the
same process, blind to the confidence it had first given each line, and that second reading kept 56 of 85; no one
else has checked them. %% KB §69.51, §69.52, §69.53, §69.55, §69.105

\subsection{What the thermal pair can and cannot do} \label{sec:thermal}

Inside one area the diurnal-contrast index behaves as a material signal should. On the object-based classes of Ius
Chasma (Section~\ref{sec:segments}), the steep canyon wall (mean slope $21.5^{\circ}$) and the mantled chasma floor ($17.0^{\circ}$) are the two
steepest units, yet differ by 0.46 in the index at opposite signs, and fresh crater ejecta come out damped against the
plateau (Figure~\ref{fig:iusindex}). The nine classes run from $-0.467$ on wall outcrop (2.6\% of the window) and
$-0.443$ on the lower wall and floor bedrock (3.8\%) to $+0.202$ on the dust-mantled plateau, which is 43.4\% of the
window, and $+0.261$ on the highest ground (3.4\%). The order is what the canyon's materials would give, rock damped
and mantle swinging, and slope does not produce it: the index correlates with slope at $-0.248$, and the wall and the
floor take opposite signs at nearly one steepness. That is as far as the evidence goes. Nothing calibrated resolves
these units, and at the 3~km the Thermal Emission Spectrometer does resolve, the index does not follow inertia in
this window (below). %% KB §24.3, §42.4

Across \pmsixty\ the signal does not survive (Figure~\ref{fig:provinces}). Over seven provinces whose thermal-inertia

province. The sources show why: every province reproduces the mosaic-wide THEMIS mean and spread
($125.75\pm34.36$~DN by day), so dusty and rocky provinces differ by $-0.19$~DN by day and $+0.02$~DN by night, while
Viking red, which is not stretched that way, separates them by 47.67~DN. TES measures a fivefold contrast between the
same groups (dayside medians of 45 and 246 tiu). The THEMIS DN are local contrast, not radiance. %% KB §28.10, §41

\subsection{Craters} \label{sec:craters}

The basin pass finds 217,622 closed depressions at 1.6~km in 48~s, 5,144 of them $\ge20$~km, including Hellas
(2,697~km across, 7,857~m deep), Utopia (2,093~km) and Isidis (1,026~km). Of the 117 named IAU craters $\ge100$~km
inside $\pm58^{\circ}$, 88 (75\%) have a closed depression near their centre and 79 (68\%) one whose diameter is within
50\%; on those the median diameter error is $+2.2\%$ (10th and 90th percentiles $-12.6\%$ and $+20.8\%$)
(Figure~\ref{fig:basins}). H4 named image gradients, not fill depth, so it was tested as written as well: around each of
the 117 craters the mean gradient of an input in rings 0.05 crater radii wide, a rim found when the strongest ring
between 0.5 and 1.5 radii lies at 0.8 to 1.2 and stands out from the rings inside and outside it by more than at
95\% of 1,000 rings of the same sizes placed at random, so that chance is under 5\%. On the 109 craters with the
whole ring in the stack, the gradient of the five spectral bands together finds 104 (95.4\%, chance 4.7\%), its rim
peaks at a median 0.975 radii; each Viking band finds 101, day infrared 103, slope 104, and night infrared only 38.
H4 holds as stated, and image gradients find rims that fill depth misses because a breached crater's walls still
shade. They do so largely through topography: the Viking mosaic was normalised so equal slopes show equal contrast,
day infrared carries the heating of sunward slopes, and the least topographic band, night infrared, finds a third.
Below 100~km the same rule on 400 Robbins craters drawn at random per size bin finds 62.3\% of the rims of 20--30~km
craters, 77.3\% at 30--50~km and 79.4\% at 50--100~km, against 4.3 to 4.7\% by chance; slope finds as many or more,
night infrared 26.4 to 34.6\%, and the misses are the degraded rims, found at 52 to 69\% where the freshest are found
at 85 to 100\%. The basins follow the dichotomy: they crowd
the southern highlands and Arabia Terra and thin out over Tharsis, Elysium and the northern plains, as the cratering
record does. A red ring with no blue symbol inside it is a named crater the pass missed, most of them degraded or
breached. %% KB §28.11, §69.132, §69.140

100~m elevation model, the thermal response is each candidate's index as a z-score against 200,000 background pixels

to Strahler order 2 and higher (1,009 candidates at Ius, 1,803 at Athabasca), the medians are 3.8 and

on all candidates and $-0.08$ ($-0.20$ to $+0.04$) at order 2 and higher, and neither window's candidates differ from
their own background (mean z $+0.04$ and $+0.03$): channels routed on the elevation model carry no thermal signature
of their own in these mosaics. %% KB §51.1

200~m elevation within 1~km of the drawn line, and cut into 10~km pieces; the thermal response is each piece's mean
index as a z-score against its own block, and intervals come from resampling whole lines 2,000 times, so the pieces of

rilles are steeper by median, 8.70 against 5.45~m~km$^{-1}$, the direction H2 names, but the difference, $+3.25$ ($-3.20$

part still fails. Seven rilles could have resolved a difference of about twice the one observed: a shift of

\subsection{Lava flows}

The classification's lava-tube class does not find lava plains. Against SIM~3292 unit groups, expressed as enrichment
$P(\mathrm{class}\mid\mathrm{group})/P(\mathrm{class})$ (Figure~\ref{fig:t8}), lava tube is slightly depleted on volcanic
plains ($\times0.84$) and enriched on volcanic edifices ($\times3.30$) and aprons ($\times4.29$): steep volcano flanks.
Normal Ground covers 76\% of the volcanic plains. The other classes sit where the geologic map says they should, so the
map is coherent as a terrain map but is not a lava-flow map. %% KB §43.2, §57.1

Whether a class taught from the geologic map would do better was tested four ways before any map was rerun
(Figure~\ref{fig:relabel}), with the Gaussian classifier of test T1 at 1.6~km on the held-out blocks and volcanic
samples taken from SIM~3292's volcanic units outside every hand-drawn polygon. With the four hand-drawn classes (A)
the Crater, hills and Normal Ground polygons score 70.2\%, and lava tube is found on 36\% of its own held-out area.
Renaming lava tube to a volcanic class drawn from the map (B) leaves the three at 70.2\% and gives a poor class
(producer's accuracy 21\%, user's 30\%): 75\% of the volcanic plains come out Normal Ground, because in these bands a
lava plain is not separable from any other plain. Dropping lava tube altogether (C) raises the three to 79.0\%, so
the class costs the others accuracy even where it is hand-drawn. Keeping the four and adding the map's volcanic class
as a fifth (D), which the author allowed if it helped, lowers the three to 64.6\%, and only 30\% of what the fifth class calls
volcanic is, below its 38\% share of the test blocks. No fifth class was added and no map rerun; lava flows in this
study come from the geologic map's volcanic units. %% KB §43.3

\subsection{Deep learning} \label{sec:dl}

The RetinaNet detector found 41.9\% of held-out Crater polygons with 87.6\% of its in-polygon boxes correct (always
naming Crater gives 71.1\%) and 22.7\% of Normal Ground polygons at 56.9\% precision, but never named hills or lava
tube (Figure~\ref{fig:dl}, left). Retrained with the backbone unfrozen for 30 epochs and with extra chips of the rare
classes, it named hills (25.0\% recall, 100\% precision) but lost Normal Ground (4.5\% recall) and over-predicted lava
tube on craters; by the rule written before the run it did not improve. Boxes suit compact objects \citep{Lee2019}
rather than area classes, and the whole-chip boxes of plains limit this model. %% KB §66.2, §66.5

The U-Net is a different matter. In the held-out windows it scored 98.75\%, \kap\,0.977, and over all of \pmsixty\
98.8\%, but with seams at its 256-pixel tiles (phase ratio 8.6--9.2 against about 1.0 for the support vector machine
maps). The second, offset pass lowered the ratio to 1.2 and changed nothing else measured: 98.9\%, \kap\,0.980 held out,
with lava tube at 77.1\% user's accuracy against 2.4\% (Figure~\ref{fig:dl}, right, and Figure~\ref{fig:unet}). The map
is a map of terrain provinces, not of features: the southern highlands are Crater, the northern lowlands Normal Ground,
lava tube lies on Tharsis and Elysium and hills along Valles Marineris; it makes 55.1\% of \pmsixty\ Crater and
39.3\% Normal Ground, where the support vector machine map makes 33.9\% and 54.7\%. It labels 91.3\% of the ground inside named
craters as Crater, but also 82.6\% of the ring around them, and elevation bins reproduce 68.0\% of it. The held-out
labels mark areas that are mostly one class at 50~km, and a model that sees 51~km of context reproduces those areas; the
score measures agreement with the hand-drawn areas at province scale. Of the four-class maps the support vector machine map is
the landform map, with the U-Net map beside it as a province map. %% KB §66.6, §66.7, §66.8, §66.9

Retrained on the seven classes (17,444 chips, 6.2~h), the U-Net scores 69.0\%, \kap\,0.606 on the v2 held-out
polygons, against 44.1\%, \kap\,0.301 for the four-class U-Net and 42.5\% for the seven-class support vector machine
(Figure~\ref{fig:unet7}). Over the four-class U-Net, as for the support vector machine, the gain is in overall accuracy
and macro F1 (56.6\% against 37.1\%), not in balanced accuracy (56.9\% against 56.7\%). Its lead over the seven-class
support vector machine is resolved by balanced accuracy on these polygons, 56.9\% against 41.9\% (block interval $+2.3$ to $+19.5$ points), but not by accuracy ($-9.7$ to $+47.8$): most of the 26.5 points lie
in the two Tharsis blocks, 47.5\% of the held-out area, where the support vector machine scores 16.0\% and 25.6\% and
the U-Net 68.6\% and 96.3\%. On the 1,652 held-out polygons of the third label set, in the same blocks, both maps score about 3 points
lower (65.8\% and 39.8\%) and the order holds, but the balanced-accuracy lead, 52.9\% against 40.4\%, is no longer
resolved by block ($-3.7$ to $+16.3$ points): the lead is clear by polygon and rests on a few blocks. %% KB §69.57, D35
The four-class U-Net's lead over the support vector machine map holds by block ($+11.1$ to $+41.0$).
Each new class has a user's accuracy above its share of the held-out area: volcanic %% KB §69.44

\subsection{The third label set: the lead maps} \label{sec:v3}

On the 237 polygons the U-Net on the third label set scores 87.6\%, \kap\,0.844, balanced accuracy 84.5\%, against
69.0\%, 0.606 and 56.9\%; the support vector machine scores 39.1\%, \kap\,0.288, balanced accuracy 45.6\%, against
42.5\%, 0.310 and 41.9\% (Table~\ref{tab:svm}, Figures~\ref{fig:landforms8} and~\ref{fig:unet8}). By block the U-Net's
difference runs from $+13.0$ to $+38.7$ points of balanced accuracy, which is resolved; the support vector machine's,
$-2.7$ to $+8.7$, is not, nor is its fall in accuracy ($-9.7$ to $+2.3$). On the 1,652 polygons they score 86.5\% and
36.5\%, against 65.8\% and 39.8\%. The U-Net leads the nearest training polygon by $+31.3$ to $+51.8$ points of accuracy
on the 237 polygons; the support vector machine does not separate from it ($-24.9$ to $+24.3$). Volcanic edifice
covers 18.0\% of \pmsixty{} on the new U-Net map, against 40.0\%, and the two new maps agree on 47.5\% of \pmsixty,
against 39.7\%. %% KB §69.77, §69.80, §69.81, §69.82

The score was above the range registered for it (60--78\%), so a leak was sought first: no held-out polygon shares
measurable area with a training polygon, where the same test finds 89.2\% of the second label set's training area under
the third's. The gain is broad: 13 of the 14 blocks rise and 183 polygons gain against 38 that lose. Of its 18.6
points Crater gives 8.6 and Normal Ground 5.6, as the earlier model's calling Crater edifice or apron, and Normal Ground
edifice, nearly stops. The Tharsis block with 29.7\% of the pixels gains least and reads 77.2\%. %% KB §69.80

Two retrainings set out before they ran split the U-Net's gain (Figure~\ref{fig:extraruns}). Trained on the cut pieces alone (7,401 polygons),
adding nothing, it reaches a balanced accuracy of 78.1\% on the 237 polygons; on the second label set plus the added
polygons alone (3,602), cutting nothing, 77.6\%; against 84.7\% with both and 56.9\% with neither. Each half gives
part of the gain, about equally (their difference, $-7.8$ to $+5.8$ points, is not resolved by block), and the two
together most. By pixel accuracy the added polygons alone score highest, 89.3\%, but lose hills ($-37.4$ points) and
lava tube ($-38.6$): most of their hills are the rule's. %% KB §69.88, §69.93

same seven bands scored at 30,000 points in the 237 polygons, repeats its direction: 39.3\% trained on the second
label set, 38.8\% on the third, at 1,000 pixels a class. Ten times the 1,000 pixels a class that Pro draws changes its
accuracy by $-0.6$ to $+1.0$ points, and the third label set does not raise it ($-7.5$ to $+3.1$): what holds the
support vector machine is one pixel's seven bands, not its sample or its labels. The third set moved its boundary
between Crater and hills. The rule's hills, half of them on crater flanks, are harder to tell from Crater by those
bands (two-class accuracy 0.722 against 0.877), and Pro's map on that set reads 21.0\% of the held-out Crater as
hills, against 4.9\%: Crater, the larger class, loses what hills gains, so accuracy falls while balanced accuracy
rises. Given each pixel's surroundings as well, each band's mean and spread over 1, 5 and 20~km, the stand-in rises
from 39.2\% to 57.1\% on the third label set ($+9.7$ to $+28.2$ points) and to 60.9\% on the second, so the third
set still does not help it ($-9.6$ to $-0.2$); the U-Net, at 88.2\% on the same points, leads it by $+12.0$ to

U-Net still leads by $+9.7$ to $+37.1$, while the third label set still does not help it: what the U-Net draws
from that set is learnt spatial pattern, not band statistics over its view. Of its 26.2-point lead at those points,
apron gives 14.85 and Crater 8.48, classes defined by where they lie, around an edifice or inside a rim; the
stand-in does better on hills and fluvial channel. Given the point's map coordinates as well it reaches 69.2\%,
and the U-Net still leads by 19.0 points. %% KB §69.95, §69.97, §69.98, §69.100

\subsection{Where the two methods agree} \label{sec:agree}

A score on held-out polygons says how each map does where a class was drawn; where the two methods give the same
class says how far either can be relied on elsewhere (Figure~\ref{fig:agree}). The published four-class map and the
four-class U-Net agree on 61.4\% of \pmsixty. On the second label set the seven-class pair agrees on 39.7\%, and on
the third, the lead pair, on 47.5\%. Three predictions were written before the first comparison and held: under 75\%
for four classes, under 50\% for seven, and more agreement on the held-out polygons than over \pmsixty\ and more
again on the training polygons (73.6\% and 86.5\% for four classes, 43.2\% and 54.6\% for seven). The last did not
hold for the lead pair, which agrees on 40.5\% of the held-out polygons' ground, less than over \pmsixty, and on
48.3\% of the training polygons'. %% KB §69.46, §69.81

What the two methods share is the plains. Normal Ground's intersection over union rises from 0.42 to 0.64 between
the second and third label sets, while Crater's falls from 0.30 to 0.12: the two lead maps disagree most where the
support vector machine draws fluvial channel and the U-Net Crater, 10.7\% of \pmsixty. The support vector machine
maps Crater on 5.4\% of \pmsixty\ and fluvial channel on 31.9\%; the U-Net 27.8\% and 8.8\%. For four classes the
agreement runs from 90.9\% at 50--60\textdegree{}N to 50.2\% at 30--40\textdegree{}S, and for the seven classes of
the second label set from 67.8\% to 25.4\% at 50--60\textdegree{}S: the maps agree on the smooth northern plains and
part company on the cratered highlands. A class boundary on either lead map is therefore one method's reading; where both give
the same class, 47.5\% of the extent, the reading does not depend on the method. %% KB §69.46, §69.81

\subsection{A probability sample} \label{sec:sample}

A probability sample \citep{Olofsson2014} gives scores free of where the polygons were drawn. Twenty-five points were
drawn in each class of the seven-class support vector machine map, with probability proportional to area, and 35
more inside the author's held-out polygons were hidden among them as a check on the labelling; an automated process
labelled all 210 blind on Context Camera images and elevation-model shading, with no map class shown. On the hidden
points it agreed with the author's classes on 66.7\% of the 27 it decided ($\kappa=0.604$), short of the 70\% set in
advance, and missed the apron and fluvial polygons, so what follows is the automated reading, reported at the
author's decision. On the 142 sample points it decided, by the stratified estimator, the published four-class map
scores 56.9\% (48.8--65.0\%), the four-class U-Net 44.4\% (35.8--52.9\%), the seven-class support vector machine
39.9\% (35.6--44.2\%) and the seven-class U-Net 21.7\% (13.7--29.7\%), each below its score on the held-out polygons.
On the same points the seven-class U-Net minus the seven-class support vector machine is $-18.3$ points ($-26.7$ to
$-9.9$), the reverse of its lead on the polygons, but the labelling's lean to Normal Ground favours the support vector
machine, which maps more of it. On the 57 points not read as Normal Ground the difference is $-4.1$ points ($-27.9$ to
$+19.8$), so the sample does not rank those two maps.
Normal Ground covers 79.8\% of the sampled ground (73.4--86.3\%) against 33.3\% of the seven-class map, and fluvial
channel, a third of the map, 3.3\% (a user's accuracy of 4.8\%). The labelling named none of the 175 sample points apron, its
weakest class on the check, so the apron share is not measured. %% KB §69.53, §69.55, §69.77

The points were drawn before the third label set, so they are not its own. The U-Net on it scores 49.6\%
(40.8--58.5\%), $+28.0$ points on its predecessor ($+17.5$ to $+38.4$), and the support vector machine 40.2\%
(34.3--46.1\%), $+0.3$ ($-5.4$ to $+5.9$). The lean does not explain the U-Net's gain: on the 57 points not read as
Normal Ground it scores 56.4\% (36.8--75.9\%) against 28.7\%, a difference of $+27.7$ ($+12.3$ to $+43.1$). The U-Net

\subsection{Whether the composite's classes repeat (H3)} \label{sec:h3}

the two maps were compared by the adjusted Rand index \citep{Hubert1985}, which is 1 for identical partitions and 0
for chance agreement. Coherence, the share of four-neighbour pixel pairs that fall in one class, was measured beside
it (Table~\ref{tab:t6}, Figure~\ref{fig:t6}). %% KB §47.3

absence is not evidence against: the composite's classes are the ones that follow the canyon walls, floor and plateau.

\subsection{The question at Athabasca Valles} \label{sec:athabasca}

The introduction asked whether the stack can tell lava, water and impact landforms apart at Athabasca Valles, and
how much of the answer the thermal infrared carries. In this stack the flood lava is told apart by three things, and
none of them is thermal. The first is the geologic map: the channel floor lies in unit \lAv, and no band reproduces
that unit's contacts or the margins traced on Context Camera images much above chance (day infrared separates 19.7\%
of the profiles across the traced margins and Viking red 14.8\%, where chance is 10\%). The second is crater density
from the catalogue, which puts \lAv\ lowest of the window's units, at 1,122 craters $\ge1$~km per $10^6$~km$^2$
against 1,760 and 2,183; the closed-depression detector reverses that order by counting pits on flat lava, so the
evidence of youth comes from the catalogue and not from this study's detector. The third is form: the window's
channel candidates are shallow, a median of 1.4~m~km$^{-1}$ against 10.2 at Ius Chasma, which is the setting of a
lava plain and says nothing of what cut the channels. %% KB §42.2, §47.2, §51.1, §69.52

The thermal pair adds least exactly here. The diurnal-contrast index does not follow calibrated inertia in the
window ($r=-0.01$) while Viking colour does ($-0.64$); the window's $5^{\circ}$ blocks lie at the weak end of the
planet for night infrared ($+0.06$ to $+0.16$); and the channel candidates carry no thermal signature against their
own background. So the answer to the question as posed is no for these mosaics: they do not separate lava from
water-cut ground at Athabasca Valles, and what the study can say of the flood lava it takes from the map and the
crater catalogue. That is a statement about 8-bit display mosaics stretched region by region, not about thermal
infrared, and the calibrated products named under Future work are the test the question deserves.
%% KB §42.4, §51.1, §69.30

\subsection{The thermal infrared at four distances} \label{sec:scales}

What the thermal pair is worth depends on the distance it is asked at. At one pixel it adds little: 1.4 to 2.0
points of accuracy to the four-class support vector machine, and none by balanced accuracy. Inside one area it
orders surfaces as their materials would: the nine classes of Ius Chasma run from $-0.467$ on wall outcrop to
$+0.261$ on the highest ground, and slope does not produce that order. Against calibrated inertia the signal is
real, weak and local, of the physical sign in 97\% of the $5^{\circ}$ blocks for night infrared at
$|r|\approx0.1$, and gone across the planet, where dusty and rocky provinces differ by $-0.19$ and $+0.02$~DN.
With 51~km of surroundings the bands carry 8.0 points for a support vector machine and 6.4 to 7.6 for the U-Net,
but map coordinates given in their place recover most or all of that, as a contrast stretch set region by region
would have it: at that distance the mosaics tell a classifier where it is. These are one fact seen four times. The
mosaics keep contrast within a region and discard it between regions, so they sharpen boundaries that a classifier
already has from terrain and cannot, alone, say what a surface is made of. Classes of calibrated inertia used as
labels show the same: the pair adds half a point within blocks and nothing resolved across them, and
nothing to duricrust, the unit only inertia can tell from dust.
%% KB §24.3, §28.10, §43.1, §56.1, §69.29, §69.86, §69.99--§69.101, §69.133, §69.134

\subsection{What the thermal bands carry: calibrated material labels} \label{sec:material}

The landform labels cannot say whether the thermal bands map materials, since slope and relief predict them alone.
The geologic map's unit groups \citep{Tanaka2014} are no material
labels either: on a 4~km lattice of the stack they are predicted no better than by their commonest group, with or
without the thermal bands, which add at most a point. A measured material property does better as a label. On the
same lattice and held-out blocks, TES nightside inertia \citep{Putzig2007} in three classes fixed before the run
(below 150, 150 to 349, and 350~tiu and above, where TES measured) is predicted at 59.3\% balanced accuracy, against
33.3\% for chance. Without the Viking bands that falls 8.5 points; without the thermal bands, 0.4 (interval 0.0 to
1.2), with accuracy $+0.5$ ($-0.0$ to $+1.1$), so by the rule they do not help across blocks. Within blocks they add
0.5 points of accuracy and 0.6 balanced, both resolved. The thermal pair alone reaches 44.5\%, so it holds some of
the material signal, nearly all of it also in the Viking colour. Crossing inertia with TES albedo
\citep{Christensen2001} at 0.20 gives the thermophysical units of \citet{Mellon2000}: dust (low inertia, bright),
duricrust (high inertia, bright) and sand and rock (high inertia, dark). Duricrust is the test case, since only inertia
tells it from dust. The stack predicts the three units at 68.7\% balanced accuracy across held-out blocks, and leaving
out Viking costs 26.1 points; leaving out the thermal bands costs 1.5 points of accuracy ($+0.1$ to $+3.4$) and 0.9
balanced ($-0.3$ to $+2.3$), so again they do not help across blocks by the rule, and duricrust, found 53.5\% of the
time, gains nothing from them ($-0.7$, interval $-3.9$ to $+2.2$). On two calibrated label sets, then, the display
mosaics add about a point and do not carry the inertia contrast that separates crust from dust. Neither the edges
nor the classifier decides this. Over three sets of inertia edges and four of unit edges, each with maximum likelihood
and with gradient-boosted trees, the thermal bands add 0.26 to 1.85 points of accuracy across held-out blocks and pass
the rule in 2 of the 14 runs, both on one setting (inertia classes split at 100 and 300~tiu); the trees score higher
on every label set, by 2.5 to 7.7 balanced points, but not through the thermal pair, and duricrust gains at most 1.7
points from it. The landform maps themselves carry little of this material information: crossed with the units on the
TES grid (Figure~\ref{fig:mapsunits}), the U-Net's classes remove 4.5\% of the uncertainty about a cell's unit and the support vector machine's
6.4\% (Cram\'er's $V$ 0.220 and 0.257, the difference resolved, narrowly). Where they lean, the lean is physical:
fluvial channels and craters sit on dark, high-inertia ground, the U-Net's apron and lava tube on dust (median inertia
94 and 78~tiu). %% KB §69.133--§69.136

A third calibrated label, independent of inertia and albedo, gives the same answer. The TES dust cover index
\citep{Ruff2002}, read from its colour steps into dusty (below 0.94), transitional and clear ($\ge0.96$) ground
before any model ran, agrees with the thermophysical units without being the same label (Cram\'er's $V$ 0.629). The
stack predicts it at 58.9\% balanced accuracy across held-out blocks; leaving out the Viking bands costs 18.8
points, the thermal bands 0.2 (accuracy $+0.1$, interval $-0.1$ to $+0.3$), so they do not help by the rule; within
blocks they add half a point. Three calibrated label sets, then, agree (Figure~\ref{fig:material}a).
%% KB §69.142

Where, then, is the inertia contrast the night mosaic ought to hold? It is local. Within 2$^{\circ}$ windows the night
mosaic is warmer on duricrust than on dust, with the physical sign in 77\% of them, but weakly (area under the curve
0.556, where 0.5 is none); pooled over \pmsixty\ it is 0.516, while Viking red separates the two across the planet
(0.328, duricrust darker) and hardly within windows (Figure~\ref{fig:material}b). The day mosaic shows no sign of it
(0.508). The small gain the thermal bands give within blocks does not survive map coordinates either: with latitude
and longitude added to both band sets, on the inertia classes and the units, with either classifier, at either split,
the thermal bands help in none of the eight runs, where without coordinates they helped within blocks in all four.
Where they helped, they were telling the classifier where it was, as at 51~km on the landform labels
(Section~\ref{sec:v3}). %% KB §69.138, §69.139

Calibrated THEMIS inertia \citep{Fergason2006} measures this at the mosaics' own resolution. Read over the two type
areas from the 100~m quantitative mosaic, without downloading its 2.5~GB tiles whole, log inertia ranks with the night
mosaic at Spearman's $\rho=+0.603$ (Ius Chasma) and $+0.509$ (Athabasca Valles) within 10~km tiles, positive in 99\%
and 97\% of them, the strongest local relation of any input; the diurnal-contrast index is weaker, and day infrared,
Viking red and slope carry almost none within tiles. Across each whole window the night mosaic falls to $+0.442$ and
$+0.185$, and at Athabasca Viking red tracks inertia more closely ($-0.493$). The display mosaics therefore do carry
thermal inertia, as local contrast within about 10~km, and lose it beyond, where the colour of dust carries the
material signal; a classifier that must name a surface from its pixel values across regions has little to learn from
them. %% KB §69.144

\subsection{What each score measures} \label{sec:scores}

Four kinds of score appear in this paper, and they answer different questions. A held-out polygon score says how a
map does where a landform was plain enough to draw, in blocks the model never saw; it is the only score every map
has, and it flatters five of the six maps sampled. The nearest training polygon's class says how much of that score
geography alone would give: 83.2\% for four classes, more than the support vector machine map's 73.8\%, and 45.6\%
for seven, which only the seven-class U-Nets clear. Intervals from resampling whole blocks say whether a difference
would survive other ground: the lead U-Net's gain over its predecessor does ($+13.0$ to $+38.7$ points of balanced
accuracy), and the seven-class U-Net's lead in accuracy over the support vector machine does not ($-9.7$ to
$+47.8$). The probability sample says how a map does on ground chosen by area and not by whoever drew the labels,
and there the lead U-Net falls from 87.6\% to 49.6\%; its labels failed their check, so neither number is an
unbiased accuracy, but five of the six maps fall the same way. %% KB §69.35, §69.44, §69.80--§69.82

Two further measures say what a score cannot. A second training moves the lead U-Net's score by under a point and
its map on 26.8\% of the ground, and the two lead maps, made by different methods from the same labels, agree on
47.5\% of \pmsixty. Read together: the lead U-Net reproduces drawn landforms on unseen blocks far better than
geography alone, about half of the points drawn by area agree with it, and the class at any one pixel is one
training's and one method's reading. %% KB §69.81, §69.106

The intervals assume that whole 15$^{\circ}$ blocks are close to independent. On the material labels of
Section~\ref{sec:material} they are not quite: classification errors stay correlated to about 2,400~km for the inertia
classes, which are provinces, and to 800~km for the thermophysical units. Grouping the 14 held-out blocks into
30$^{\circ}$ blocks widens the accuracy interval by 4\% or narrows it by 8\% and changes no verdict, because the
held-out blocks lie apart; blocks of 5 to 7.5$^{\circ}$ would narrow the intervals by half or more and turn both
thermal verdicts to help. The 15$^{\circ}$ blocks are therefore the cautious choice, and the results that rest on
correlated samples are the gains within blocks. %% KB §69.141

\subsection{Against earlier automated mapping}

Each product here falls short of the method built for its own landform, and the comparison shows where. A
convolutional network on lunar elevation models recovers 92\% of a human-made test set's craters
\citep{Silburt2019}; fill depth on the Mars model recovers 52.4\% of the catalogued craters $\ge5$~km and does not see
degraded or breached rims, which a learned detector was not tried on here. The global valley map of \citet{Luo2009}
was found by an algorithm on topography and then edited by eye; unedited, the routed network here meets the valley
lines of \citet{Alemanno2018} no more often than the same map rotated in longitude, so the editing is the method, and
the candidate review stands in for it. DoMars16k \citep{Wilhelm2020} trains a network on 16,150 Context Camera samples
of fifteen landforms; the classes here rest on 971 hand-drawn polygons at 200~m (the third label set's 10,269
training and 1,652 held-out polygons are cut from them or placed by rule), which is why every score sits beside a
geography-only baseline and why the U-Net's lead reads as a province map. Unsupervised classes from topographic
attributes \citep{Bue2006} need no labels but name nothing; the supervised maps here name their classes and are scored
on blocks they never saw. What the stack adds is the one grid: the three families are mapped from co-registered
visible, thermal and elevation data, and the thermal bands' share is measured rather than assumed: for the support
vector machine small (1.4 to 2.0 points on four classes) and, by balanced accuracy, absent; for the seven-class U-Net,
larger (6.4 to 7.6 points, where a second training with them moves under one) but not resolved by block; and for a support vector machine given
the U-Net's 51~km of surroundings, 8.0 to 9.9 points and resolved, all of it in the surroundings and most or all
of it recovered by the point's map coordinates instead, so it is not shown to be material.
%% KB §69.40, §69.50 (W3), §69.86, §69.99, §69.100, §69.101

\subsection{Limits}

The central limit is in the data held. The THEMIS mosaics used here are display products, stretched region by region,
so they carry local contrast and texture; planet-wide material discrimination needs calibrated products such as the
TES inertia map, at the cost of resolution (3~km), or THEMIS-derived inertia \citep{Fergason2006} where it exists. In
this study the Viking colour carries the regional material signal, and it is not calibrated albedo.

The labels limit what the classification can show. The four hand-drawn classes are landforms, and slope and relief
alone predict them as well as the full stack. Calibrated material labels are better, and what the thermal bands add to them is the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:material}: about a point, none of it resolved across held-out blocks once location is known.
%% KB §69.133--§69.144
Lava tube, with 44 polygons, is too thin a class for a planet-scale map; the U-Net makes it
usable only as a province.

An audit of the 512 labels found no two classes sharing ground, but five Crater polygons drawn around volcanoes (Arsia,
Pavonis and Ascraeus Mons, Tharsis and Ulysses Tholus) and six lava-tube polygons beside fluvial valleys. Arsia Mons
alone is 31\% of the held-out Crater area, so part of the Crater score, and of the U-Net's Crater province, measures
volcanic shields. A seven-class scheme follows from the audit: Crater narrowed to impact craters, with volcanic edifice,
apron and fluvial channel as new classes. Its 971 labels (734 training, 237 held out in the same 14 blocks) were
applied on 27 October. Every relabel, check and new edifice outline was reviewed by eye; the 172 apron and 276
fluvial-strip proposals were screened by a rule written before it ran (a strip kept only where its median height lies
at least 20~m below that of a 4.8~km flank ring and under half of it lies on volcanic plains; an apron only where its
median slope is at least $1^{\circ}$ and under half of it lies inside crater rims), not inspected one by one; and
hills fall short of the training minimum, in two training blocks against three. Both seven-class maps failed the
rule set before they ran, on the four original classes, and are presented for what they name rather than for that
score: the support vector machine over-predicts fluvial channel and the U-Net volcanic edifice. On the third label
set the U-Net's edifice falls to 18.0\% of \pmsixty, while the support vector machine's fluvial channel stays at 31.9\%.
Off Tharsis the edifice class is weak: on six polygons an automated process drew at the calderas of Nili and Meroe Paterae
on Syrtis Major, in a held-out block, the U-Net calls 7.7\% of the ground volcanic edifice (1.4--20.5\%,
resampling the polygons) and the support vector machine 0.6\%, so the U-Net's edifice score is Tharsis ground;
the process could not tell lobate debris aprons from talus on the views it had, so apron was not scored there.
%% KB §68.1--§68.4, §69.8, §69.24--§69.26, §69.81, §69.105

The detectors are elevation-model products. Closed depressions miss breached craters by construction, and below about
5~km their density follows the sharpness of the HRSC/MOLA blend. Channels inherit the same dependence, and the fill
mask that removes synthetic lakes also breaks networks where the DEM is pitted.

Every polygon score in this paper is taken where a class is clear, which flatters five of the six maps sampled, all but
the support vector machine on the third label set. The probability sample shows the size of that, but on
automated labels, which failed their check; unbiased accuracies and class areas
need the same points labelled by an analyst who passes it. %% KB §69.40, §69.55
The U-Net maps are firmer as scores than as pixels: the third label set's model, run again over the held-out windows
on chips placed differently, scores 87.54\% against 87.62\% on the 237 polygons, yet about one pixel in ten changes
class, so a single pixel of a U-Net map should not be read alone. %% KB §69.83
Nor do the polygon scores fix the class areas. Trained a second time with every setting the same, the U-Net scores
within a point of the first on both polygon sets, but over the held-out windows its volcanic edifice covers 8.7\% of
the ground against 16.3\%, the difference going mostly to Crater and Normal Ground. At the 39 labelled sample points
inside the windows the second training's map calls 4 volcanic edifice against 10, nearer the automated labels' 1, too
few points to choose between them. The edifice share of \pmsixty\ (18.0\%) is one training's. Normal Ground
squares placed on the extra edifice bring it to 6.7\% off the edifice units in a training at a fixed learning rate,
at no resolved cost in score, but they move Crater, fluvial channel and apron as well, and that training was decided
after the first with the squares was read (Section~\ref{sec:v3}). %% KB §69.106, §69.125, §69.126

The held-out blocks have now been used three times, by the four-class models, the seven-class ones and those on the
third label set. That set was requested after the seven-class scores were read, so any gain on it is not a fresh test,
and its held-out crater discs come from the catalogue the crater results are checked against, so that class partly
measures agreement with it. Fourteen other blocks, drawn at random, give a retrained U-Net 86.6\%, and a second draw 85.4\%,
so its score is not tied to the first 14, though on the second its lead over the nearest training polygon is resolved
only once weighted to the first 14 blocks' class mix; the four- and seven-class scores still rest on the first 14.
%% KB §69.84, §69.96, §69.102
Any further tuning, of the deep-learning models above all, would
turn the 14 held-out blocks into a validation set; a next round needs validation blocks of its own. The retraining
against the extra edifice was judged on validation blocks of its own before the held-out ones, the first run here to
be. For its first training they gave the same verdict as the held-out blocks did afterwards; for its second they
were the stricter, 3.4 points below the control where the held-out blocks showed no loss, a reminder that one draw of
14 blocks holds its own class mix. Finally, the manual review of the 9,287 type-area candidates (2,610 and 3,283 channels, 1,685 and 1,709
depressions) has reached 3,791, with a precision (accepts over accepts and rejects) of 54\% on the 188 steep,
rock-floored Ius channels, 50\% on 209 rock-floored Athabasca channels, and 10\% and 13\% on the closed depressions
of the two areas. An automated process read the other 200 rock-floored Athabasca channels on CTX, at the author's
decision, and accepted 3 (186 rejected, 11 unsure); on 48 lines the author had reviewed, the same reading accepted 3
against the author's 22 and agreed with the author no better than chance ($\kappa=0.14$), so the channel precisions
are each reading's standard, not ground truth. The digitised classes hold 202 channels (199 accepted by the author, 3 by
the automated process) and 381 crater rims, and the 86 traced lava margins, so the machine products are presented as
candidates with measured recall and precision. %% KB §66.5, §6, §69.33, §69.52, §69.55, NEXT-STEPS §6

\subsection{Future work}

Five steps would move these results furthest. The probability sample needs labels from an analyst who passes its
check (Section~\ref{sec:sample}), to give unbiased accuracies and class areas with intervals, free of where the
polygons were drawn. Material labels from photo interpretation, rather than landforms, unit groups or inertia
classes or thermophysical units, would test what the thermal bands add to mapping geologic materials, and THEMIS-derived inertia \citep{Fergason2006}, where it exists,
would replace the display mosaics' local contrast. A check of the lava margins traced on Context Camera images
\citep{Malin2007} by an analyst would make H1's test independent, and rilles mapped at a finer scale would give H2's
second stage more than seven volcanic lines. The retraining judged on validation blocks of its own (Section~\ref{sec:v3}) shows the form the next should take,
with the learning rate fixed and not left to the tool, as in its second training, so that only the labels change, and
with predictions written for that training before it runs; the models also need labels
checked by eye rather than placed by rule: the lead U-Net's lava tube (user's accuracy 8.0\%) and its volcanic
edifice, denser inside none of ten small named edifices, come first. For the craters, a rim-based diameter or merged
nested basins would recover the 23\% of missed craters that hold only a smaller closed basin, 72\% of the
misses at $\ge50$~km.

\section{Conclusions} \label{sec:conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Four global products now share one co-registered grid over \pmsixty, 86.6\% of Mars, with the THEMIS pair never
resampled and the elevation model reproduced exactly at 200~m.
\item Viking colour, day infrared and night infrared span about three dimensions locally, night infrared nearly independent of the others, but the held THEMIS
mosaics carry local contrast only; calibrated thermal inertia follows Viking colour across the planet. Their gradients
mark the geologic map's unit contacts only faintly, the five bands together a little more than any one (Q6).
On classes of TES inertia, thermophysical units and TES dust cover as labels, the thermal pair adds at most 1.85
points across held-out blocks and passes the rule in 2 of 15 runs, and with map coordinates in none; duricrust,
which only inertia tells from dust, gains nothing. Against 100~m THEMIS inertia the night mosaic ranks the ground at
$\rho=+0.603$ and $+0.509$ within 10~km tiles but $+0.442$ and $+0.185$ across the two type areas: it holds inertia
as local contrast, which a classifier across regions cannot use.
\item Image gradients find the rims of 104 of 109 large named craters (H4), largely through the topography the
Viking and day-infrared mosaics carry, against 38 for night infrared; fill depth recovers 68\% of them and gives 465,354 closed depressions $\ge1$~km whose precision
against Robbins rises from 15.3\% to 63.2\% at 5~km; recall, 52.4\% at 5~km, falls with rim degradation, from 83.1\%
for the freshest rims to 38.4\% for the most degraded, and breached craters stay invisible. Below 100~km image
gradients still find 62.3 to 79.4\% of the Robbins rims, fewer as craters shrink and degrade.
\item Routed channels are dominated by the elevation model at \pmsixty\ (89.8\% under $2^{\circ}$) and meet the mapped
valleys no more often than a rotated valley map; stream order is a parameter, not a measurement.
\item The four-class landform map scores a balanced accuracy of 66.7\% (73.8\%, \kap\,0.58 overall) on blocks it never
saw, where geography alone, the nearest training polygon's class, gives 61.3\% (83.2\% overall); the thermal bands add
a resolved 1.4 to 2.0 points of overall accuracy and none by balanced accuracy. A seven-class U-Net trained once
without them scores 6.4 to 7.6 points lower, a difference not resolved by block but larger than a second
training's under one point; a support vector machine given 51~km
of surroundings loses 8.0 points without them, a resolved difference that the point's map coordinates largely make up. A U-Net reaches 98.9\% as a
terrain-province map.
\item Revised to seven classes, the maps separate impact craters from volcanoes and name volcanic edifices, aprons
and fluvial channels: the U-Net scored 69.0\%, \kap\,0.606 and the support vector machine 42.5\% on 237 held-out
polygons, below the four-class maps on the original classes.
\item On a third label set of more and smaller polygons the U-Net scores 87.6\%, \kap\,0.844 on the same polygons and
49.6\% against 21.7\% on a probability sample, a resolved gain on both, though the sample's labels failed their check and
the set was asked for after the earlier scores were read; on two draws of 14 other blocks, held out from retrained models,
it scores 86.6\% and 85.4\%, and its gain over the second label set comes about equally from cutting the polygons and from adding
new ones. The support vector machine gains nothing resolved (39.1\%,
\kap\,0.288; 40.2\% on the sample) and fails the rule set for it, held by one pixel's seven bands rather than by its
sample of 1,000 pixels a class or by its labels; both lead this paper. A retraining of the U-Net with Normal Ground
squares on its extra volcanic edifice failed four of five predictions (73.0\% on the 1,652 polygons) in a training
that went unlike the others; trained again at the control's learning rate it keeps its score (88.5\% and 86.4\%)
and brings the extra edifice from 15.4\% to 6.7\% of the ground off the edifice units, but misses the validation bound
by 0.4 points and halves Crater, so both are reported as tests.
\item Lava flows are not separable in these bands; the geologic map remains the lava-flow reference; H1 is not supported on 1,476~km of margins traced on CTX either, and H2 is not supported on the geologic map's own rilles and channels either.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The rules of the third label set} \label{app:rules}

The third label set (Section~\ref{sec:v3}) holds 11,921 polygons, 10,269 for training and 1,652 held out. No one
accepted or rejected a polygon by eye: each row was passed or failed by a rule written into the script before it ran,
and the rules are given here in full so that the set can be rebuilt or changed. Two kinds of polygon enter it. A
\emph{piece} is part of a polygon of the second label set: every such polygon over 1,500~km$^2$ or wider than
$1^{\circ}$ is cut by a $0.5^{\circ}$ grid, pieces under 25~km$^2$ are dropped, and a piece is kept if it passes the
screen of its class; smaller polygons are kept whole and unscreened. A piece keeps its parent's side of the split, so
no held-out ground of the second set becomes training ground. A \emph{new polygon} is placed from reference data
alone, never on the ground of any polygon of the second set, kept or dropped, and one cell clear of every other new
polygon; it goes to training or held out by the $15^{\circ}$ block of its centre, and its number is capped in each
block. %% KB §69.49

The measures come from three sources. The 200~m elevation model is summarised in cells of $16\times16$ pixels
(3.2~km): the median and the 90th-percentile slope, and the 5th, 50th and 95th-percentile height. The crater database
\citep{Robbins2012} is drawn as discs on an 800~m grid at 1.0 crater radius, the rim test, and at 1.3 radii for craters
of 3--100~km, the exclusion; ``crater cover'' is the share of a polygon inside those discs. The geologic map
\citep{Tanaka2014} gives each cell a unit kind: edifice, apron, landslide, volcanic plains or highland.
Table~\ref{tab:rules} counts the polygons each class gave; the screen of a cut piece and the rule that places a new
polygon, with its cap in a $15^{\circ}$ block, follow class by class. %% KB §69.49

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Crater.} A piece is kept if at least 0.5 of it lies inside the rims of catalogued craters $\ge4$~km. A new
polygon is a catalogued crater's disc at the rim, one in each diameter bin (6--12, 12--25, 25--50 and 50--120~km) in a
block, placed if the rim stands at least 50~m, or 5~m per km of diameter if that is more, above the floor.
\item \emph{Steep/windy hills.} A piece is kept if the median of its cells' 90th-percentile slope is $\ge8^{\circ}$ and
under half of it lies on edifice, apron or landslide units. A new polygon is $3\times3$ cells with at least 7 of 9 at a
90th-percentile slope $\ge10^{\circ}$, relief $\ge1{,}000$~m and no crater $\ge10$~km within 1.3 radii, off those
units; 4 a block.
\item \emph{Lava tube.} Polygons are kept whole, since no data screens a piece of a pit chain. New polygons are
troughs in the mapped extent of the 16 named catenae: a black top-hat of the elevation model (4.2~km closing) at
least 50~m deep, with an elongation $\ge3$ and a length $\ge6$~km, outside crater rims; at most 15 a catena.
\item \emph{Normal Ground.} A piece is kept if its median slope is $\le2^{\circ}$, its crater cover $\le0.3$ and it
lies off those units; craters of 8--100~km are then cut out at 1.2 radii, and half the piece must remain. A new
polygon is $4\times4$ cells, every cell at a median slope $\le1^{\circ}$, a 90th-percentile slope $\le3^{\circ}$ and
relief $\le150$~m, with no crater $\ge4$~km within 1.3 radii, off those units; 5 a block.
\item \emph{Volcanic edifice.} A piece is kept if it lies on edifice units or a named volcano and slopes
$\ge1^{\circ}$, unless it is within 0.3 of the volcano's radius, where calderas are flat. A new polygon is $4\times4$
cells of edifice units, every cell sloping $\ge1^{\circ}$, with no crater $\ge10$~km within 1.3 radii; 12 a block.
\item \emph{Apron.} A piece is kept if it lies on apron units and slopes $\ge0.7^{\circ}$; poleward of $30^{\circ}$ its
cells' 90th-percentile slope must also be $\le15^{\circ}$, which keeps mesa scarps out. A new polygon is $4\times4$
cells, at least 14 of 16 passing the same tests; 12 a block.
\item \emph{Fluvial channel.} A piece is kept if its floor lies at least 20~m below a flank ring 4.8~km out and under
half of it lies on volcanic units. New polygons are strips 20~km long and 2.4~km wide along every second segment of
the mapped valleys \citep{Alemanno2018}, under the same floor rule; 2 a valley and 6 a block.
\end{enumerate}

The screens rejected 161 Crater pieces that lay outside rims, 345 edifice, 146 apron, 47 Normal Ground and 910 fluvial
pieces, and 680 new valley strips. Of 1,049 craters measured for a new disc 693 were placed, of 1,269 valley strips
589, and of 592 troughs 119. Against the second label set the hills train in 150 blocks where they trained in 2, and
the lava tubes in 11 where they trained in 3; the edifice polygons still train in 23 blocks and are held out in 3,
which is why their score is Tharsis ground (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). %% KB §69.49

Two rules were changed after the first polygons were read on hillshade sheets and before the set that stands was
written. The first run placed 315 lava-tube chains from closed depressions outside crater rims; at full resolution
none showed a pit, so the catena troughs replaced them. The cap on the cells' 90th-percentile slope had rejected the
ridged aureole lobes of Olympus Mons, which the class key names as apron, so it now applies poleward of $30^{\circ}$
only. One rule was changed after a check of the screened rows: the slope test rejected the flat fan-shaped deposits on the west
flanks of the Tharsis volcanoes, so those deposits pass without it, which added 106 apron polygons, all mostly on that unit.
